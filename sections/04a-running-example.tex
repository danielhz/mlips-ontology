\subsection{Running Example: Moment Tensor Potentials for Ti-Al}
\label{sec:running-example}

We introduce the ontology through a concrete example: training a Moment
Tensor Potential (MTP)~\cite{shapeev2016mtp,novikov2021mlip} for the
titanium-aluminium (Ti-Al) system, a binary alloy (a metallic material
composed of two chemical elements). This example illustrates
how the ontology captures the full lifecycle of an MLIP study---from the
underlying physics, through the algorithm's hyperparameters and training
data, to the evaluation of the trained model.

\paragraph{The simulation problem.}
Consider a \emph{simulation cell}---a finite region of space containing
atoms, with periodic boundary conditions that tile the cell infinitely
in all directions to approximate a bulk material. The cell contains $n$ atoms at positions
$\relPos_1, \ldots, \relPos_n$ with atomic types
$\atomType_1, \ldots, \atomType_n$ (where $\atomType_i \in \{\mathrm{Ti}, \mathrm{Al}\}$ for our
Ti-Al system). The goal of an interatomic potential is to predict the
total energy $E$ and the forces $\mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\relPos_i} E$
acting on each atom, given only the atomic positions and types.
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-cell} illustrates the simulation cell and the
concept of atomic neighborhoods.

In quantum mechanics, the total energy can be computed exactly (within
the approximations of density functional theory) by solving the
Kohn--Sham equations~\cite{kohn1965dft}. However, this is
computationally expensive---scaling as $O(n^3)$ with the number of
atoms---limiting DFT to systems of a few hundred atoms. Machine learning
interatomic potentials approximate the DFT energy surface at a fraction
of the cost, enabling simulations of millions of atoms.

\paragraph{The MTP algorithm.}
In the MTP framework~\cite{shapeev2016mtp}, the total energy of an
atomic configuration $\config$ is decomposed into local contributions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-energy}
  \totalEnergy(\config) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \localEnergy(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\neigh{i}$ is the \emph{neighborhood} of atom~$i$, defined as
the set of all atoms within the cutoff radius $\Rcut$. The
neighborhood consists of the atomic type $\atomType_i$ of the central atom, and
for each neighbor $j$ within $\Rcut$: the atomic type $\atomType_j$ and
the relative position vector $\relPos_{ij} = \relPos_j -
\relPos_i$. Crucially, atoms beyond $\Rcut$ do not
contribute to $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$---this \emph{locality assumption} is
what makes the potential computationally efficient.

The local energy $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$ is expressed as a linear combination
of basis functions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-basis}
  \localEnergy(\neigh{i}) = \sum_{\alpha} \xi_\alpha \, \basisFunc_\alpha(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are the \emph{moment tensor descriptors}---invariant
functions of the neighborhood that capture the local atomic
environment---and $\fitParams = \{\xi_\alpha\}$ are the
\emph{parameters to be learned} by fitting to DFT reference data. The
basis functions $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are constructed from contractions of moment
tensors $M_{\mu,\nu}$ involving radial functions $\radialBasis^{(\beta)}(|\relPos_{ij}|)$
that vanish smoothly at $\Rcut$.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
The MTP algorithm has several hyperparameters that control the model's
expressiveness and computational cost:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Rcut$ (\emph{cutoff radius}): determines the size of
  atomic neighborhoods. A larger cutoff captures longer-range
  interactions but increases computational cost.
\item $\Rmin$ (\emph{minimum distance}): the shortest expected
  interatomic distance, used to define the radial basis domain.
\item $\nRadialBasis$ (\emph{number of radial basis functions}): controls the
  resolution of the radial description.
\item $\levmax$ (\emph{maximum level}): determines which
  basis functions $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are included, controlling the trade-off
  between accuracy and model size.
\end{itemize}
%
These are \emph{not} learned from data---they are chosen by the
researcher before training. In contrast, the coefficients
$\fitParams$ are determined by fitting
Equation~\eqref{eq:mtp-basis} to DFT-computed energies, forces, and
stresses via linear regression.

\paragraph{Encoding the algorithm.}
The MTP algorithm and its hyperparameters map directly to the
\MLIPsOntology{}. The algorithm is an instance of $\MLIPAlgorithmC$, and
each hyperparameter is an instance of $\HyperparameterC$ with its name,
data type, and valid range:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={MTP algorithm and its hyperparameters in RDF.},label=lst:mtp-algorithm]
ex:MTP a mlips:MLIPAlgorithm ;
    rdfs:label "Moment Tensor Potential" ;
    mlips:hasHyperparameter ex:hp-rcut, ex:hp-rmin,
        ex:hp-nq, ex:hp-levmax ;
    mlips:supportsSimulation ex:sim-md, ex:sim-geopt .

ex:hp-rcut a mlips:Hyperparameter ;
    mlips:hyperparameterName "R_cut" ;
    mlips:hyperparameterDatatype "float" ;
    mlips:defaultValue "5.0" ;
    mlips:minValue "2.0" ;
    mlips:maxValue "10.0" .

ex:hp-levmax a mlips:Hyperparameter ;
    mlips:hyperparameterName "lev_max" ;
    mlips:hyperparameterDatatype "integer" ;
    mlips:defaultValue "16" .
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Training data.}
The ground truth for training comes from DFT calculations: for a set of
atomic configurations of the Ti-Al system, one computes the total energy,
forces on each atom, and (optionally) stresses. Each configuration is a
snapshot of atomic positions in a periodic cell, generated by sampling
from molecular dynamics trajectories, random perturbations, or active
learning~\cite{novikov2021mlip}. A typical training dataset for Ti-Al
might contain 15{,}000 configurations computed with the PBE
exchange-correlation functional, PAW pseudopotentials, and a plane-wave
energy cutoff of 520~eV using the VASP code.

\paragraph{Encoding the training data.}
The training dataset, its material system, and the DFT calculation
settings are encoded as follows:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Training dataset and DFT settings in RDF.},label=lst:mtp-training]
ex:ds-TiAl a mlips:TrainingDataset ;
    rdfs:label "Ti-Al DFT dataset" ;
    mlips:coversMaterial ex:mat-TiAl ;
    mlips:coversProperty mlips:Energy, mlips:Forces, mlips:Stresses ;
    mlips:datasetProvenance mlips:Published ;
    mlips:numConfigurations 15000 ;
    mlips:hasDFTCalculation ex:dft-TiAl .

ex:mat-TiAl a mlips:MaterialSystem ;
    mlips:chemicalFormula "TiAl" ;
    mlips:materialClass "binary alloy" .

ex:dft-TiAl a mlips:DFTCalculation ;
    mlips:hasDFTSettings ex:dft-settings-TiAl .

ex:dft-settings-TiAl a mlips:DFTSettings ;
    mlips:xcFunctional "PBE" ;
    mlips:pseudopotentialType "PAW" ;
    mlips:energyCutoff 520.0 ;
    mlips:kPointMesh "4x4x4" .
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Encoding the training run and evaluation.}
A $\TrainingRunC$ records the concrete training event: it $\executesR$
the MTP algorithm, $\runsOnR$ the Ti-Al dataset, and $\producesR$ a
$\TrainedModelC$---the specific set of coefficients $\fitParams$
fitted to this data. The concrete hyperparameter values used
($\Rcut = 5.0$~\AA{}, $\levmax = 16$, $\nRadialBasis = 8$) are
recorded as $\HyperparameterSettingC$ instances. When the trained model
is evaluated on a test set, the evaluation is captured as a
$\BenchmarkResultC$:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Training run, trained model, and benchmark result in RDF.},label=lst:mtp-benchmark]
ex:run-mtp-TiAl a mlips:TrainingRun ;
    mlips:executes ex:MTP ;
    mlips:runsOn ex:ds-TiAl ;
    mlips:produces ex:model-mtp-TiAl .

ex:model-mtp-TiAl a mlips:TrainedModel ;
    rdfs:label "MTP for Ti-Al (lev16, rcut5)" .

ex:study-smith2025 a mlips:BenchmarkStudy ;
    mlips:hasResult ex:result-01 .

ex:result-01 a mlips:BenchmarkResult ;
    mlips:evaluatesModel ex:model-mtp-TiAl ;
    mlips:targetMaterial ex:mat-TiAl ;
    mlips:hasAccuracyMetric ex:metric-rmse-energy .

ex:metric-rmse-energy a mlips:AccuracyMetric ;
    mlips:metricType mlips:RMSE ;
    mlips:metricProperty mlips:EnergyProperty ;
    mlips:metricValue 1.2 .
\end{lstlisting}
