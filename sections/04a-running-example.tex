\subsection{Running Example: Moment Tensor Potentials for TiCr$_2$ Laves Phases}
\label{sec:running-example}

We introduce the ontology through a concrete published study: training a
Moment Tensor Potential (MTP)~\cite{shapeev2016mtp,novikov2021mlip} for
hydrogen absorption in the TiCr$_2$--H system by Kumar et
al.~\cite{kumar2025ticr2h}. TiCr$_2$ is an \emph{intermetallic
compound}---a metallic material with fixed AB$_2$ composition formed by
Ti and Cr---and is a candidate hydrogen-storage material. It crystallises
in two close-packed forms (a cubic one and a hexagonal one), and Kumar
et al.\ train one MTP per form over the hydrogen-concentration range
$0 < x \le 6$ in TiCr$_2$H$_x$. We use the cubic form (called \emph{C15}
in the materials literature) as the running example. The example
illustrates how the ontology captures the full lifecycle of an MLIP
study---from the underlying physics, through the method's
hyperparameters and training data, to the evaluation of the trained
model.

\paragraph{The simulation problem.}
Consider a \emph{simulation cell}---a finite region of space containing
atoms, with periodic boundary conditions that tile the cell infinitely
in all directions to approximate a bulk material. The cell contains $n$
atoms at positions $\relPos_1, \ldots, \relPos_n$ with atomic types
$\atomType_1, \ldots, \atomType_n$ (where
$\atomType_i \in \{\mathrm{Ti}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{H}\}$ for our
TiCr$_2$H$_x$ system). The goal of an interatomic potential is to
predict the total energy $E$ and the forces
$\mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\relPos_i} E$ acting on each atom, given only
the atomic positions and types. Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-cell}
illustrates the simulation cell and the concept of atomic neighborhoods.

In quantum mechanics, the total energy can be computed exactly (within
the approximations of density functional theory) by solving the
Kohn--Sham equations~\cite{kohn1965dft}. However, this is
computationally expensive---scaling as $O(n^3)$ with the number of
atoms---limiting DFT to systems of a few hundred atoms. Machine learning
interatomic potentials approximate the DFT energy surface at a fraction
of the cost, enabling simulations of millions of atoms.

\paragraph{The MTP method.}
In the MTP framework~\cite{shapeev2016mtp}, the total energy of an
atomic configuration $\config$ is decomposed into local contributions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-energy}
  \totalEnergy(\config) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \localEnergy(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\neigh{i}$ is the \emph{neighborhood} of atom~$i$, defined as
the set of all atoms within the cutoff radius $\Rcut$. The
neighborhood consists of the atomic type $\atomType_i$ of the central
atom, and for each neighbor $j$ within $\Rcut$: the atomic type
$\atomType_j$ and the relative position vector
$\relPos_{ij} = \relPos_j - \relPos_i$. Crucially, atoms beyond $\Rcut$
do not contribute to $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$---this \emph{locality
assumption} is what makes the potential computationally efficient.

The local energy $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$ is expressed as a linear
combination of basis functions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-basis}
  \localEnergy(\neigh{i}) = \sum_{\alpha} \xi_\alpha \, \basisFunc_\alpha(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are the \emph{moment tensor
descriptors}---invariant functions of the neighborhood that capture the
local atomic environment---and $\fitParams = \{\xi_\alpha\}$ are the
\emph{parameters to be learned} by fitting to DFT reference data. The
basis functions $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are constructed from contractions of
moment tensors $M_{\mu,\nu}$ involving radial functions
$\radialBasis^{(\beta)}(|\relPos_{ij}|)$ that vanish smoothly at
$\Rcut$.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter definitions vs.\ learned parameters.}
A key distinction in the MLIP workflow---and in the ontology---is between
\emph{hyperparameter definitions}, \emph{hyperparameter settings}, and
\emph{learned parameters}:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item A \emph{hyperparameter definition} declares a parameter that the
  method accepts: its name, data type, valid range, and default value.
  For example, MTP defines a hyperparameter ``$\Rcut$'' of type float,
  with range $[2.0, 10.0]$~\AA{} and default $5.0$~\AA{}. These
  definitions are properties of the \emph{method}, independent of any
  specific training.
\item A \emph{hyperparameter setting} is a concrete value chosen by the
  researcher for a specific MLIP run: e.g., $\Rcut = 5.0$~\AA{},
  $\levmax = 16$. These are properties of the \emph{MLIP run}.
\item The \emph{learned parameters} $\fitParams = \{\xi_\alpha\}$ are
  determined during training by fitting
  Equation~\eqref{eq:mtp-basis} to DFT data. The number of parameters
  depends on the hyperparameter settings (e.g., a higher $\levmax$
  includes more basis functions and thus more coefficients). These
  define the \emph{trained model}.
\end{itemize}
%
The MTP method defines four hyperparameters:
$\Rcut$ (cutoff radius, controlling neighborhood size),
$\Rmin$ (minimum interatomic distance, defining the radial basis domain),
$\nRadialBasis$ (number of radial basis functions), and
$\levmax$ (maximum level, controlling model complexity).

To support domain queries such as ``which physical hyperparameters
does this method accept?'', we classify hyperparameters into three
non-disjoint subclasses of $\HyperparameterC$: physical (direct physical
meaning), architectural (model-architecture choice), and training
(training-procedure parameter).
These subclasses are \emph{not disjoint}: many MLIP hyperparameters
straddle categories. For MTP, $\Rcut$ and $\Rmin$ are physical (they bound
interaction ranges); a pure training hyperparameter is the optimizer's
learning rate; but $\nRadialBasis$ and $\levmax$ are both architectural
\emph{and} physical---they control the model size but also determine how
finely the radial distance and the angular moment expansion (e.g., dipole,
quadrupole) are resolved. Acknowledging this overlap is a modelling choice
informed by domain expertise: a strict partition would misrepresent how
materials scientists actually think about these parameters, while the
annotation itself enables physics-focused queries that a flat
$\HyperparameterC$ class cannot answer directly.

\paragraph{Encoding: from trained model to method.}
The ontology captures this three-level distinction.
We present the encoding bottom-up, starting from the trained model---the
artifact a researcher has in hand---and tracing back to the method
definition.

A $\TrainedModelC$ is the concrete result of training: the fitted
coefficients $\fitParams$. It is linked to an $\MLIPRunC$ that
records which method was applied, with which hyperparameter settings,
on which dataset:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={A trained model and its MLIP run.},label=lst:trained-model]
ex:model-mtp-TiCr2H-c15 a mlips:TrainedModel ;
    rdfs:label "C15-MTP for TiCr2-H (lev16, rcut5)" .

ex:run-mtp-TiCr2H-c15 a mlips:MLIPRun ;
    mlips:appliesMethod ex:MTP ;
    mlips:runsOn ex:ds-TiCr2H-c15 ;
    mlips:produces ex:model-mtp-TiCr2H-c15 .
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The hyperparameter settings for this MLIP run are instances of
$\HyperparameterSettingC$, each linking to the hyperparameter
definition it instantiates:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Hyperparameter settings for a specific MLIP run.},label=lst:hp-settings]
ex:setting-rcut a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-rcut ;
    mlips:settingValue "5.0" ;
    mlips:hasUnit unit:ANGSTROM .

ex:setting-levmax a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-levmax ;
    mlips:settingValue "16" .

ex:setting-nq a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-nq ;
    mlips:settingValue "8" .
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The hyperparameter \emph{definitions} live at the method level. They
describe what parameters the method accepts, not what values were
used in any particular MLIP run. The method also specifies its three
components (functional form, loss function, training algorithm),
declares which simulation types it supports, and identifies the
software that implements it:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={MTP method: components, hyperparameter definitions, and implementation.},label=lst:algorithm]

\paragraph{Training data.}
The ground truth for learning $\fitParams$ comes from DFT calculations:
for a set of atomic configurations of TiCr$_2$H$_x$, one computes the
total energy, forces on each atom, and (optionally) stresses. Each
configuration is a snapshot of atomic positions in a periodic cell,
generated by sampling from molecular dynamics trajectories, random
perturbations, or active learning~\cite{novikov2021mlip}. Kumar et
al.~\cite{kumar2025ticr2h} construct the C15 training set in
multiple active-learning steps; the initial low-hydrogen-concentration
enumeration alone yields 1{,}019 configurations on
$2 \times 2 \times 2$ supercells of the primitive C15 unit cell (48 metal atoms),
computed with the PBE exchange-correlation functional, PAW
pseudopotentials, and a plane-wave energy cutoff of 400~eV using the
VASP code:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Training dataset and DFT settings.},label=lst:training-data]
ex:ds-TiCr2H-c15 a mlips:TrainingDataset ;
    rdfs:label "TiCr2-H DFT dataset (C15, step 1)" ;
    mlips:coversMaterial ex:mat-TiCr2 ;
    mlips:coversProperty mlips:Energy, mlips:Forces, mlips:Stresses ;
    mlips:datasetProvenance mlips:Published ;
    mlips:numConfigurations 1019 ;
    mlips:hasDFTCalculation ex:dft-TiCr2H .

ex:mat-TiCr2 a mlips:MaterialSystem ;
    mlips:chemicalFormula "TiCr2" ;
    mlips:materialClass "Laves phase" .

ex:dft-TiCr2H a mlips:DFTCalculation ;
    mlips:hasDFTSettings ex:dft-settings-TiCr2H .

ex:dft-settings-TiCr2H a mlips:DFTSettings ;
    mlips:xcFunctional "PBE" ;
    mlips:pseudopotentialType "PAW" ;
    mlips:energyCutoff 400.0 ;
    mlips:kPointMesh "4x4x4" .
\end{lstlisting}
