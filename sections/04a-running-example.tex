\subsection{Running Example: Moment Tensor Potentials for Ti-Al}
\label{sec:running-example}

We introduce the ontology through a concrete example: training a Moment
Tensor Potential (MTP)~\cite{shapeev2016mtp,novikov2021mlip} for the
titanium-aluminium (Ti-Al) system, a binary alloy (a metallic material
composed of two chemical elements). This example illustrates
how the ontology captures the full lifecycle of an MLIP study---from the
underlying physics, through the algorithm's hyperparameters and training
data, to the evaluation of the trained model.

\paragraph{The simulation problem.}
Consider a \emph{simulation cell}---a finite region of space containing
atoms, with periodic boundary conditions that tile the cell infinitely
in all directions to approximate a bulk material. The cell contains $n$
atoms at positions $\relPos_1, \ldots, \relPos_n$ with atomic types
$\atomType_1, \ldots, \atomType_n$ (where
$\atomType_i \in \{\mathrm{Ti}, \mathrm{Al}\}$ for our Ti-Al system).
The goal of an interatomic potential is to predict the total energy $E$
and the forces $\mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\relPos_i} E$ acting on each
atom, given only the atomic positions and types.
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-cell} illustrates the simulation cell and the
concept of atomic neighborhoods.

In quantum mechanics, the total energy can be computed exactly (within
the approximations of density functional theory) by solving the
Kohn--Sham equations~\cite{kohn1965dft}. However, this is
computationally expensive---scaling as $O(n^3)$ with the number of
atoms---limiting DFT to systems of a few hundred atoms. Machine learning
interatomic potentials approximate the DFT energy surface at a fraction
of the cost, enabling simulations of millions of atoms.

\paragraph{The MTP algorithm.}
In the MTP framework~\cite{shapeev2016mtp}, the total energy of an
atomic configuration $\config$ is decomposed into local contributions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-energy}
  \totalEnergy(\config) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \localEnergy(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\neigh{i}$ is the \emph{neighborhood} of atom~$i$, defined as
the set of all atoms within the cutoff radius $\Rcut$. The
neighborhood consists of the atomic type $\atomType_i$ of the central
atom, and for each neighbor $j$ within $\Rcut$: the atomic type
$\atomType_j$ and the relative position vector
$\relPos_{ij} = \relPos_j - \relPos_i$. Crucially, atoms beyond $\Rcut$
do not contribute to $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$---this \emph{locality
assumption} is what makes the potential computationally efficient.

The local energy $\localEnergy(\neigh{i})$ is expressed as a linear
combination of basis functions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mtp-basis}
  \localEnergy(\neigh{i}) = \sum_{\alpha} \xi_\alpha \, \basisFunc_\alpha(\neigh{i}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are the \emph{moment tensor
descriptors}---invariant functions of the neighborhood that capture the
local atomic environment---and $\fitParams = \{\xi_\alpha\}$ are the
\emph{parameters to be learned} by fitting to DFT reference data. The
basis functions $\basisFunc_\alpha$ are constructed from contractions of
moment tensors $M_{\mu,\nu}$ involving radial functions
$\radialBasis^{(\beta)}(|\relPos_{ij}|)$ that vanish smoothly at
$\Rcut$.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter definitions vs.\ learned parameters.}
A key distinction in the MLIP workflow---and in the ontology---is between
\emph{hyperparameter definitions}, \emph{hyperparameter settings}, and
\emph{learned parameters}:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item A \emph{hyperparameter definition} declares a parameter that the
  algorithm accepts: its name, data type, valid range, and default value.
  For example, MTP defines a hyperparameter ``$\Rcut$'' of type float,
  with range $[2.0, 10.0]$~\AA{} and default $5.0$~\AA{}. These
  definitions are properties of the \emph{algorithm}, independent of any
  specific training.
\item A \emph{hyperparameter setting} is a concrete value chosen by the
  researcher for a specific training run: e.g., $\Rcut = 5.0$~\AA{},
  $\levmax = 16$. These are properties of the \emph{training run}.
\item The \emph{learned parameters} $\fitParams = \{\xi_\alpha\}$ are
  determined during training by fitting
  Equation~\eqref{eq:mtp-basis} to DFT data. The number of parameters
  depends on the hyperparameter settings (e.g., a higher $\levmax$
  includes more basis functions and thus more coefficients). These
  define the \emph{trained model}.
\end{itemize}
%
The MTP algorithm defines four hyperparameters:
$\Rcut$ (cutoff radius, controlling neighborhood size),
$\Rmin$ (minimum interatomic distance, defining the radial basis domain),
$\nRadialBasis$ (number of radial basis functions), and
$\levmax$ (maximum level, controlling model complexity).

\paragraph{Encoding: from trained model to algorithm.}
The ontology captures this three-level distinction.
We present the encoding bottom-up, starting from the trained model---the
artifact a researcher has in hand---and tracing back to the algorithm
definition.

A $\TrainedModelC$ is the concrete result of training: the fitted
coefficients $\fitParams$. It is linked to a $\TrainingRunC$ that
records which algorithm was used, with which hyperparameter settings,
on which dataset:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={A trained model and its training run.},label=lst:trained-model]
ex:model-mtp-TiAl a mlips:TrainedModel ;
    rdfs:label "MTP for Ti-Al (lev16, rcut5)" .

ex:run-mtp-TiAl a mlips:TrainingRun ;
    mlips:executes ex:MTP ;
    mlips:runsOn ex:ds-TiAl ;
    mlips:produces ex:model-mtp-TiAl .
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The hyperparameter settings used in this training run are instances of
$\HyperparameterSettingC$, each referencing the hyperparameter
definition it instantiates:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Hyperparameter settings for a specific training run.},label=lst:hp-settings]
ex:setting-rcut a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-rcut ;
    mlips:settingValue "5.0" ;
    mlips:hasUnit unit:ANGSTROM .

ex:setting-levmax a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-levmax ;
    mlips:settingValue "16" .

ex:setting-nq a mlips:HyperparameterSetting ;
    mlips:forHyperparameter ex:hp-nq ;
    mlips:settingValue "8" .
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The hyperparameter \emph{definitions} live at the algorithm level. They
describe what parameters the algorithm accepts, not what values were
used in any particular training run. The algorithm also declares which
simulation types it supports and which software implements it:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={MTP algorithm: hyperparameter definitions and implementation.},label=lst:algorithm]
ex:MTP a mlips:MLIPAlgorithm ;
    rdfs:label "Moment Tensor Potential" ;
    mlips:hasHyperparameter ex:hp-rcut, ex:hp-rmin,
        ex:hp-nq, ex:hp-levmax ;
    mlips:hasImplementation ex:mlip-package-v1 ;
    mlips:supportsSimulation ex:sim-md, ex:sim-geopt .

ex:hp-rcut a mlips:Hyperparameter ;
    mlips:hyperparameterName "R_cut" ;
    mlips:hyperparameterDatatype "float" ;
    mlips:defaultValue "5.0" ;
    mlips:minValue "2.0" ; mlips:maxValue "10.0" .

ex:hp-levmax a mlips:Hyperparameter ;
    mlips:hyperparameterName "lev_max" ;
    mlips:hyperparameterDatatype "integer" ;
    mlips:defaultValue "16" .

ex:mlip-package-v1 a mlips:Implementation ;
    mlips:implementedIn ex:lib-mlip ;
    mlips:version "1.0.0" .

ex:lib-mlip a mlips:Library ;
    rdfs:label "MLIP package" .
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Training data.}
The ground truth for learning $\fitParams$ comes from DFT calculations:
for a set of atomic configurations of the Ti-Al system, one computes the
total energy, forces on each atom, and (optionally) stresses. Each
configuration is a snapshot of atomic positions in a periodic cell,
generated by sampling from molecular dynamics trajectories, random
perturbations, or active learning~\cite{novikov2021mlip}. A typical
training dataset for Ti-Al might contain 15{,}000 configurations
computed with the PBE exchange-correlation functional, PAW
pseudopotentials, and a plane-wave energy cutoff of 520~eV using the
VASP code:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Training dataset and DFT settings.},label=lst:training-data]
ex:ds-TiAl a mlips:TrainingDataset ;
    rdfs:label "Ti-Al DFT dataset" ;
    mlips:coversMaterial ex:mat-TiAl ;
    mlips:coversProperty mlips:Energy, mlips:Forces, mlips:Stresses ;
    mlips:datasetProvenance mlips:Published ;
    mlips:numConfigurations 15000 ;
    mlips:hasDFTCalculation ex:dft-TiAl .

ex:mat-TiAl a mlips:MaterialSystem ;
    mlips:chemicalFormula "TiAl" ;
    mlips:materialClass "binary alloy" .

ex:dft-TiAl a mlips:DFTCalculation ;
    mlips:hasDFTSettings ex:dft-settings-TiAl .

ex:dft-settings-TiAl a mlips:DFTSettings ;
    mlips:xcFunctional "PBE" ;
    mlips:pseudopotentialType "PAW" ;
    mlips:energyCutoff 520.0 ;
    mlips:kPointMesh "4x4x4" .
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
When the trained model is evaluated on a test set, the evaluation is
captured as a $\BenchmarkResultC$ within a $\BenchmarkStudyC$:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=turtle,caption={Benchmark study and evaluation result.},label=lst:benchmark]
ex:study-smith2025 a mlips:BenchmarkStudy ;
    mlips:hasResult ex:result-01 .

ex:result-01 a mlips:BenchmarkResult ;
    mlips:evaluatesModel ex:model-mtp-TiAl ;
    mlips:targetMaterial ex:mat-TiAl ;
    mlips:hasAccuracyMetric ex:metric-rmse-energy .

ex:metric-rmse-energy a mlips:AccuracyMetric ;
    mlips:metricType mlips:RMSE ;
    mlips:metricProperty mlips:EnergyProperty ;
    mlips:metricValue 1.2 .
\end{lstlisting}
